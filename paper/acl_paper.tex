\begin{abstract}
Thai is written without spaces between words, and its spelling depends on tone marks, silent
consonants, and the transliteration of many loanwords. Each of these is a common source of
misspellings. We present \textbf{ThaiEditBench}, a benchmark for correcting Thai spelling errors
at the character level. It has three components: (i) a \textbf{six-class taxonomy} that types
each error by the part of the spelling that goes wrong, plus an etymology tag; (ii) \textbf{three
length tiers} (sentence, paragraph, and page) that test whether a model stays accurate over long,
mostly correct text; and (iii) a \textbf{contamination-robust construction}, in which naturally
attested misspellings are \emph{injected} into clean text, so the corrupted passages do not
appear verbatim in the source corpora.
Because the errors are injected, the gold corrections are exact, and a blind two-annotator study
measures how reliably the taxonomy can be applied. Grading is at the level of Thai character
clusters, with span-based matching adapted from ERRANT. We evaluate \textbf{16 models} and find
that (1) under the benchmark's conditions, a model that locates an error almost always fixes it
correctly, so what separates models is \emph{detection}; (2) the evaluated Thai-adapted models do
not lead the strongest general models; and (3) in long, mostly correct passages, weaker models
accumulate over-edits that pull their scores far below their sentence-level results. On real errors
mined from Wikipedia edit history, scores are somewhat lower, but the model ranking largely
holds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Thai spelling goes wrong in ways that are specific to its script. Writers choose the wrong tone
mark, add or drop the mark that silences a consonant, confuse consonants or vowel forms that
sound alike, reorder the letters of a consonant cluster, and transliterate loanwords
inconsistently. For instance, สังเกต (`to observe') is often written สังเกตุ, with an extra vowel
mark. These are \textbf{character-level} errors, and because Thai has no spaces between words,
they are hard to locate and score with word-based tools. However, the resources to study them are
missing. Grammatical error correction (GEC) benchmarks are largely English
\citep{bryant-etal-2017-automatic,bryant-etal-2019-bea,napoles-etal-2017-jfleg}, and the closest
character-level task is Chinese spelling check (CSC)
\citep{tseng-etal-2015-introduction,liu-etal-2011-visually}. Thai error-annotated resources
cover social-media, informal, or learner text
\citep{lertpiya-etal-2020-thai,nakwijit-purver-2022-misspelling}. Document-level GEC studies
correction over long context \citep{yuan-bryant-2021-document}, but no benchmark tests how Thai
spelling correction holds up over long passages that contain few errors.

This work addresses this gap with a formal-register benchmark that types each error by how it
arises and that limits contamination. We score at the level of the Thai Character Cluster (TCC)
\citep{theeramunkong-etal-2000-character}, a group of characters, such as a consonant with its
vowel and tone marks, that Thai writing rules do not allow to be split. Our work contributes the
following.
(1)~\textbf{ThaiEditBench}, a character-level benchmark for Thai spelling correction. It types
each error with one of six \emph{mechanism} classes, the part of the spelling that goes wrong,
plus an etymology tag, and it has \textbf{three length tiers} (sentence, paragraph, and page). It
is \textbf{contamination-robust by construction}: we inject naturally attested misspellings into
clean text, so the corrupted passages are new and the gold corrections are exact. A blind
annotation study measures how reliably the taxonomy can be applied (Krippendorff's
$\alpha = 0.75$).
(2)~An \textbf{ERRANT-style edit-typer for Thai}. It splits the difference between an input and a
correction into TCC-level edits and assigns each edit one of the six classes where one applies. This lets us report
\textbf{span-F0.5} separately for each error type, as ChERRANT does for Chinese
\citep{zhang-etal-2022-mucgec}. We use it to score and diagnose systems on this benchmark, not as
a general Thai grammatical error classifier.
(3)~A \textbf{16-model evaluation}. Models that find an error almost always fix it, so detection
separates them. The evaluated Thai-adapted systems do not lead, weaker models accumulate
over-edits in long text, and the ranking largely holds on naturally occurring errors
(Spearman $\rho = 0.88$).
We release the code, data, grader, edit-typer, and variant map at
\url{https://github.com/yongsa-nut/ThaiEditBench}.

\section{Related Work}

\noindent\textbf{GEC and CSC.} English grammatical error correction is evaluated with
span-based metrics. These metrics represent a correction as a set of edits, each a span of the
input and its replacement, and credit a system for each gold edit it reproduces with the same
replacement. The M\textsuperscript{2} scorer matches a system's edits against gold edits
\citep{dahlmeier-ng-2012-better}. ERRANT (the ERRor ANnotation Toolkit) extracts the edits
automatically by aligning the input with each correction, assigns each edit an error type, and
reports span-F0.5, an F-score over matched edits that weights precision twice as much as recall
\citep{bryant-etal-2017-automatic}. GLEU instead measures fluency
\citep{napoles-etal-2015-ground}. We adapt ERRANT-style matching to TCC units
(Section~\ref{sec:typer}). Character-level Chinese spelling check is the closest task, scoring
position-based detection and correction F1 without word boundaries
\citep{tseng-etal-2015-introduction,wu-etal-2013-chinese,yu-etal-2014-overview}, with
confusion-set construction \citep{liu-etal-2011-visually} and the MuCGEC/ChERRANT
dual-granularity scorer \citep{zhang-etal-2022-mucgec}. ERRANT has been ported to Arabic
\citep{belkebir-habash-2021-automatic} and Korean \citep{yoon-etal-2023-towards}, but not Thai.

\noindent\textbf{Thai NLP.} We build on PyThaiNLP for tokenization and dictionary tooling
\citep{phatthiyaphaibun-etal-2023-pythainlp} and on TCC segmentation
\citep{theeramunkong-etal-2000-character}. Prior Thai spelling correction
targets social text \citep{lertpiya-etal-2020-thai,lertpiya-etal-2023-progressively},
including the VISTEC-TP-TH-2021 Twitter corpus with misspelling and word-segmentation
annotation \citep{limkonchotiwat-etal-2021-handling}, and \citet{nakwijit-purver-2022-misspelling}
study misspelling semantics.

\section{The ThaiEditBench Benchmark}

\subsection{Task}
Given a Thai passage containing character-level orthographic errors, return the passage with
those errors corrected and \textbf{nothing else changed}: no rewriting, re-spacing, numeral,
or punctuation edits. We score under a strict \textbf{minimal-edit contract}, where any change
to a correct span is an \emph{over-edit} and is penalized.

\subsection{Mechanism taxonomy}
We define six orthographic mechanism classes, each carrying an \textbf{etymology tag}: (1) tone
mark, (2) consonant-pair, (3) silent-consonant, (4) vowel, (5) cluster or metathesis, and
(6) loanword transliteration. Worked examples per class are in Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}. We
check canonical forms against the Royal Institute Dictionary \citep{royalinstitute-2013-dictionary}
(Appendix~\ref{app:construction}). Class~6 is governed by a
\textbf{loanword-absorption rule}: only \emph{contemporary} foreign loans are class~6, while
long-integrated borrowings (Tamil, Khmer, Persian, and Sanskrit--Pali doublets) count as native.

\subsection{Construction}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{(a) Confusion-set injection.} The confusion set holds 1{,}044 wrong$\to$correct
pairs across the six classes. Every wrong form is a \textbf{naturally attested misspelling},
harvested from the Thai Wiktionary appendix of commonly misspelled words and from fixes made by
a Thai Wikipedia spelling-cleanup bot; a rule-based classifier assigns each pair a mechanism
class (96.5\% agreement with 90 hand-labeled pairs; Appendix~\ref{app:construction}). Only the
placement is synthetic: we inject pairs into \emph{new clean text} by matching
\textbf{whole tokens}, which protects short valid forms, and record each error's TCC span,
class, and etymology. Applying
the gold edits restores the clean original for 100\% of items, so the gold is exact.
\item \textbf{(b) Cleanup-bot mining.} Real errors in their original sentences are mined from
the same bot's edit history, keeping only fixes whose corrected word is in the Royal Institute
Dictionary or PyThaiNLP word list and whose wrong and correct forms align cleanly, following the
revision-mining tradition
\citep{tanaka-etal-2020-building,grundkiewicz-junczysdowmunt-2014-wiked}, with the bot's fix as
gold. These items form a separate natural-error evaluation set (Section~\ref{sec:natural}).
\end{itemize}
\noindent\textbf{Registers.} The clean text of the evaluated tiers spans news (ThaiSum),
encyclopedic text (Thai Wikipedia), and Thai government press releases, all permissively
redistributable.

\subsection{Contamination control}
Systems are evaluated on \textbf{corrupted passages that do not appear verbatim in the source
corpora}. A residual risk remains: a model that
memorized a clean source passage could reproduce it instead of finding and fixing the injected
errors. We therefore probe memorization directly. Given the first half of 30 sentences per
source, Typhoon~2.5 (30B) continues them verbatim for only 0.3\% to 1.7\% of the true second half
on average, and never for half of it. We fill a separate held-out test split first from the
least-memorized sources (details in Appendix~\ref{app:construction}).

\subsection{Length tiers}
\label{sec:tiers}
The same pipeline produces three tiers that vary passage length and error sparsity.
\textbf{T1} (sentence) has 795 items with a mean length of 101 characters (95\% of items fall
between 43 and 174); \textbf{T2} (paragraph) has 224 items with a mean of 425 (270 to 577); and
\textbf{T3} (page) has 122 items with a mean of 1{,}242 (963 to 1{,}530). They contain 974, 278,
and 193 gold edits respectively. Injection is stratified by class
quota rather than uniform: the class mix follows the skew of the attested pairs (the per-class
share of T1 gold edits correlates with that of the attested pairs at Pearson $r = 0.80$) and is
partially flattened so that every class has at least 115 gold edits at T1
(Appendix~\ref{app:construction}). The error budget is \textbf{dense at the sentence level},
with roughly one error per 100 characters, and deliberately \textbf{sparse at paragraph and page
length}, where most sentences are clean distractors. T2 and T3 therefore measure
\textbf{precision under long correct context} (over-editing).

\subsection{Quality control}
\label{sec:qc}
Construction makes the correction of each injected error exact, but it cannot rule out two other
problems: typos already present in the source text, and words with two accepted spellings. We add
one check for each.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Cross-model consensus audit.} A typo left in the source text stays in the gold, so
a model that fixes it is wrongly scored as over-editing. We flag an item when at least $k{=}3$
systems, and at least as many as reproduce the gold, return the \emph{same} output that differs
from the gold. We keep a flagged item when that output is the unchanged input or makes only some
of the gold corrections (a \emph{shared miss}), or differs only in punctuation or spacing (a
\emph{shared over-edit}). In T2 and T3 we drop every other flagged item as a likely misspelling
left in the \emph{source}, which also drops a few items where the systems substitute an accepted
variant, a formal word, or a standard loanword spelling; T1's flagged items were judged individually. The audit removed 24 of 819 (T1), 26 of 250 (T2), and 28 of 150
(T3) items; Appendix~\ref{app:qc} describes how flagged items were checked.
\item \textbf{Variant reconciliation.} Before scoring, we map words with two accepted spellings
(four groups, eight word forms; Appendix~\ref{app:qc}) to the gold's form. No injected wrong
form belongs to the map, so the map cannot turn a missed error into a fix; it changes 2.35\% of
non-empty model outputs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Human validation of the labels}
\label{sec:annotation}
The mechanism classes are assigned automatically. To measure how reliably the taxonomy can be
applied, two native Thai speakers independently labeled 60 items (40 curated mined items and 20
synthetic ones), blind to the automatic label. Both judged all 60 marked spans to be genuine
errors, and their class agreement is Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.752$
\citep{krippendorff-2011-computing}. Where the raters agree (48 items), their class matches the
automatic label on 35; most disagreements fall at the loanword-absorption and class-2 conventions
(Appendix~\ref{app:annotation}).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Metrics}
We grade at the TCC level with ERRANT-style span matching \citep{bryant-etal-2017-automatic}.
The \textbf{primary metric is correction F1}. We derive minimal TCC edits by aligning the wrong
text with \emph{both} the gold and the system output under the \textbf{same} Levenshtein
alignment, so an output identical to the gold scores exactly 1.0, as in SIGHAN CSC scoring
\citep{tseng-etal-2015-introduction}. \textbf{Detection F1} credits the correct error site and
ignores the replacement. \textbf{Clean-sentence fidelity} is the fraction of error-free sentences
left unchanged, a precision signal for T2 and T3. The edit-typer (Section~\ref{sec:typer}) also
gives \textbf{span-F0.5}, overall and per mechanism. We report 95\% item-bootstrap confidence
intervals (Appendix~\ref{app:results}) and test differences between systems with a
\textbf{paired item-level bootstrap} \citep{koehn-2004-statistical} over 10{,}000 resamples.

\subsection{ERRANT-style edit-typer}
\label{sec:typer}
We adapt the ERRANT/ChERRANT design
\citep{bryant-etal-2017-automatic,zhang-etal-2022-mucgec} to Thai orthography. Given a (wrong,
correct) pair, the typer works in three steps. First, it extracts edits at the \textbf{TCC level},
with the same alignment the grader uses. Second, an edit often covers only part of a word, such
as a single tone mark, so the typer widens each edit to the whole word that contains it, found
with the dictionary-based word segmenter of PyThaiNLP (\texttt{newmm};
\citealp{phatthiyaphaibun-etal-2023-pythainlp}). Third, a rule-based classifier compares the
wrong and correct forms of that word character by character and assigns the class from what
changed; for example, a changed tone mark gives class~1. If the corrected word is in a loan
lexicon, the edit is class~6 (Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy} gives the full rules). The same
classifier labels the confusion set, and the typer also tags the edit operation (substitution,
insertion, deletion, transposition, or mixed). Because it types both the gold and the system edits, it gives
\textbf{span-F0.5 per mechanism} on any (wrong, correct) pair, without construction labels. On
the dev split, it recovers 98.9\% of the gold edit spans and agrees with the
construction class on 98.1\% of them. Because the typer reuses the construction-time classifier,
we treat it as a \textbf{scoring instrument} for this benchmark, not a general Thai grammatical
error classifier.

\subsection{Models and inference}
We evaluate 16 models, each in a single fixed configuration, at temperature 0 where supported,
with one fixed Thai instruction prompt. The \textbf{frontier proprietary} models are GPT-5.5
(medium and high reasoning), Gemini~3.5~Flash, Claude Opus~4.7 (non-thinking), Claude
Sonnet~4.6 (non-thinking), and GPT-5.4-mini. The \textbf{open-weight} models are Gemma-4-31B,
DeepSeek~V4 Pro and Flash, GLM-5.1, MiniMax-M2.7, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. The \textbf{Thai-adapted}
models, which adapt general base models to Thai, are Typhoon~2.5 (30B) and three ThaiLLM
8B-instruct models served through the national ThaiLLM gateway, namely OpenThaiGPT (v7.2),
Typhoon-S, and THaLLE \citep{pipatanakul-etal-2024-typhoon2,pipatanakul-etal-2026-typhoons}.
Appendix~\ref{app:prompt} gives the prompts and a prompt-sensitivity ablation.

\subsection{Where models fail: finding versus fixing}
\label{sec:detection}
We decompose every gold edit into \textbf{fixed}, \textbf{miscorrected} (the system edits
exactly the gold span but with a different replacement), and \textbf{missed} (no system edit
matches the gold span). Pooled over all 16 models, 0.866 of T1 gold edits are fixed, only 0.008
are miscorrected, and 0.126 are missed. The shares are 0.821, 0.005, and 0.173 at T3 and 0.815,
0.006, and 0.178 on natural errors (Section~\ref{sec:natural}), and no single model's
miscorrected share exceeds 0.036 on T1 (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}, Appendix~\ref{app:results}). The
pattern holds within every mechanism class, including the hardest (loanword: miscorrected 0.003
against missed 0.189). A model that rewrites a wider region around an error is scored as
missed, but such edits are only 1.3 to 1.8\% of gold edits per tier, so untouched errors still
outnumber wrong fixes at least five to one (four to one without empty replies; Limitations).
Under this benchmark's conditions (formal register, single-reference gold, and one to three errors
per passage), models rarely fail by choosing the wrong spelling once they locate an error, so
what the leaderboard separates is \textbf{detection}. Spelling knowledge still matters, since
recognizing a misspelling requires knowing the correct form; whether the finding holds for
dense, interacting errors is open (Limitations).

\subsection{Length robustness}
\label{sec:length}
Sentence-tier scores largely predict the page-tier ranking (Spearman $\rho = 0.93$), but the
\textbf{drop from T1 to T3} differs widely between models (Figure~\ref{fig:degradation}a). The
frontier loses only 0.08 to 0.14 correction F1 (GPT-5.5-med $0.969{\to}0.866$) and Gemma-4 only
0.05 ($0.950{\to}0.898$), while Typhoon~2.5 and Qwen lose 0.27 and 0.40 ($0.742{\to}0.476$ and
$0.705{\to}0.310$). Measured per 10{,}000 clean TCCs, the over-edit rate is lower at T3 than at
T1 for all 16 models (Figure~\ref{fig:degradation}b), although it rises from T2 to T3 for 11 of
them, so over-edits per passage grow mainly with the amount of correct text. What separates models is the
\emph{level} of that rate: Typhoon~2.5, OpenThaiGPT, and Qwen over-edit at 3 to 35 times the
rate of GPT-5.5 (med) at every tier (Appendix~\ref{app:results}). Clean-sentence
\textbf{fidelity} agrees: the frontier holds at or above 0.95, while the heaviest over-editors sit
at 0.77 to 0.90. These results suggest that \textbf{over-editing and missing reflect the same
weak discrimination}. At T3, even the frontier models are
limited more by over-editing than by detection: they fix 0.94 to 0.98 of gold edits, but their
precision is 0.71 to 0.80 (GPT-5.4-mini, with recall 0.85 and precision 0.88, is the exception).

\subsection{Per-mechanism difficulty}
The edit-typer types the system's edits as well as the gold's, so it separates recall from
precision for each mechanism (Table~\ref{tab:typer}). Mean recall is lowest for loanwords at T1
(0.81 against 0.86 to 0.90), and the construction labels agree (Table~\ref{tab:mech},
Appendix~\ref{app:results}); the failure is again missed rather than miscorrected. This mean
hides a split: the nine systems of the top stratum (Section~\ref{sec:leaderboard}) find 0.94 to
1.00 of loanword errors, while the four Thai-adapted models and MiniMax find 0.32 to 0.70.
Precision shows where models change text that needs no change, through \emph{unsupported} edits
that match no gold edit. Class~5 (reordered, doubled, or inserted letters) has the lowest
precision for 15 of the 16 models at T1, and at T3 class~5 or tone marks are lowest for 14 of
them. Loanword precision is high by construction: the typer assigns class~6 only when the output
word is in its loan lexicon, so a change that turns a correct loanword into another form is
usually charged to another class.

such as spacing or rewording (6\% to 84\% of a model's unsupported edits), enter no column.}

\subsection{Natural errors}
\label{sec:natural}
We evaluate all 16 systems on the mined cleanup-bot items, each a real error in its original
encyclopedic sentence. The consensus audit of Section~\ref{sec:qc} removes 16 of the 103 items,
where the agreeing systems correct more than the recorded bot fix does (Appendix~\ref{app:natural}). On the remaining 87 items, mean correction
F1 is 0.81 against 0.87 on T1, and the system ranking transfers (Spearman $\rho = 0.88$ with T1;
Table~\ref{tab:main}). Without the audit, mean F1 is 0.72, mostly because of those 16 items, and
$\rho$ is again 0.88.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented ThaiEditBench, a mechanism-typed, length-stratified benchmark for Thai spelling
correction. Across 16 models, \textbf{detection} rather than the choice of spelling separates
systems, and weaker models accumulate over-edits in long text.

\section*{Limitations}
\textbf{Sparse error density (primary).} The long tiers (T2 and T3) are deliberately sparse,
with 1 to 3 errors scattered across otherwise-clean passages, to isolate precision under long
correct context. We therefore do \textbf{not} test \textbf{high error-density} regimes such as
heavy-typo input, degraded OCR, or L2 writing, where errors co-occur, interact, mask one
another, and correction order matters. Extending ThaiEditBench to dense multi-error passages,
and testing whether the detection finding survives when errors crowd each other, is the most
important next step.

\textbf{A fixed inventory of attested errors.} Every injected error comes from 1{,}044 attested
pairs, each mapping one wrong form to one correct form. The benchmark therefore does not cover
misspellings outside the inventory, and a system that consulted the released inventory would be
answering a different, tool-augmented question; all systems are evaluated zero-shot without it.
The class mix follows the distribution of attested misspelling types, not the frequency of
errors in running text.

\textbf{Scope of the natural-error check.} The audited natural set has 87 items from one
cleanup bot in one register, with only 7 loanword and 5 class-5 edits, so it
supports the ranking and the decomposition but not per-class conclusions; loanword behaviour is
measured mainly on injected errors. The audit relies on cross-model consensus, and the bot's fix
is taken as gold, so the natural gold is less certain than the construction gold of the tiers.
A larger, naturally distributed test set, which the released mining pipeline supports, is the
main way to strengthen this check.

\textbf{Label reliability.} The annotation study covers 60 items and two raters. Per-class
agreement on ten items per class is noisy ($\alpha$ from 0.49 to 1.00) and lowest for class~6,
whose boundary depends on the absorption convention, and the automatic class matches the rater
consensus on 35 of 48 items. Per-mechanism results near the class~6 and class~2 boundaries
should be read with this uncertainty.

\textbf{Configured-system comparison.} Each system is evaluated under one fixed configuration
and one fixed Thai prompt. Reasoning modes, token budgets, provider defaults, serving stacks,
and model scale are not controlled, so the leaderboard reflects configured-system behaviour
rather than an intrinsic ranking of base-model capability. A prompt ablation on two systems
shifts correction F1 by at most 0.07 without reordering them (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}), but
we do not sweep prompts across all systems. A reply that exhausts its token budget is empty and
scored as the unchanged input, so its errors count as missed. This affects 112 of 795 T1 items
for GLM-5.1, 93 for MiniMax, and 58 for Qwen, and 51 of 122 T3 items for MiniMax; empty replies
account for 17\% of missed edits at T1 and 29\% at T3. For these systems, a low score partly
reflects failure to deliver an answer rather than failure to detect errors, and excluding empty
replies, untouched errors outnumber wrong fixes four to one rather than five to one.

\section{Mechanism taxonomy in detail}
\label{app:taxonomy}
Each class is defined by the orthographic \emph{mechanism} of the fault, not its surface
character. Multiple worked examples per class:
\begin{itemize}\raggedright\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{1 tone mark:} ค๊ะ$\to$คะ, น๊ะ$\to$นะ, จั๊กจั่น$\to$จักจั่น, กระทั้ง$\to$กระทั่ง
\item \textbf{2 consonant-pair:} กฏหมาย$\to$กฎหมาย, กบฎ$\to$กบฏ, อากาส$\to$อากาศ
\item \textbf{3 silent-consonant:} กลยุทธ$\to$กลยุทธ์, ขาดดุลย์$\to$ขาดดุล, เท่ห์$\to$เท่
\item \textbf{4 vowel:} สังเกตุ$\to$สังเกต, มาตราฐาน$\to$มาตรฐาน, ฉะเพาะ$\to$เฉพาะ
\item \textbf{5 cluster/metathesis:} กระทันหัน$\to$กะทันหัน, กระบังลม$\to$กะบังลม
\item \textbf{6 loanword:} กราฟฟิค$\to$กราฟิก, กงศุล$\to$กงสุล, ลิ๊งค์$\to$ลิงก์
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Class~2 scope.} Class~2 covers swaps within a homophone group that contains an
Indic-origin letter, for example ศ/ษ/ส, ท/ธ, and น/ณ, plus the final-stop pair บ/ป. Swaps between letters outside these
groups, such as ฝ/ฟ, fall in class~5 together with other structural edits.

\textbf{Loanword-absorption rule.} Only \emph{contemporary} foreign loans, chiefly modern
English technical and commercial terms (กราฟิก, ดิจิทัล, ลิงก์), are class~6. Long-integrated
borrowings are treated as \textbf{native} even though etymologically foreign: Portuguese
(กะละแม), Burmese (กะปิ), Mon (กะพง), Khmer (กบฏ), and Tamil, Persian, and Sanskrit--Pali
doublets. Borderline cases (e.g.\ กงสุล, \emph{consul}) are resolved by the Royal Institute Dictionary listing $+$
Wiktionary borrowed-term categories, and the tag is recorded per item so the rule is auditable.

\textbf{Automatic class assignment.} The rule-based classifier used to label the confusion set
and inside the edit-typer (Section~\ref{sec:typer}) compares the wrong and correct forms of a
word character by character. It checks, in order, for a swap within one of the homophone
consonant groups above (class~2), an added or removed silent-consonant mark ์ or a change in
the consonant it silences (class~3), reordered, doubled, or inserted letters (class~5), a
changed tone mark (class~1), and a changed vowel sign (class~4), and it assigns the first class
that applies. Any other consonant change falls in class~5. If the corrected word is in the loan
lexicon, which holds the correct forms of the class-6 confusion pairs and a few proper nouns, the
class is~6 whatever the character change.

\section{Gold quality control and the consensus audit}
\label{app:qc}
\textbf{Why construction-gold still needs a cleanup pass.} Injection guarantees that applying
the gold edits recovers the clean original (verified 100\%), so the \emph{injected} error's
correction is exact. It does \textbf{not} guarantee that the \textbf{source passage was
typo-free}: the permissive corpora contain a small rate of residual orthographic errors. When
an item's source carries such a residual typo, the gold inherits it, so a model that fixes
\emph{that} genuinely wrong span is unfairly scored as a miss or an over-edit. We find and remove
such items with a cross-model consensus audit.

\textbf{Cross-model consensus audit.} For every item we pool the system outputs
(variant-reconciled) and look for a \emph{consensus} output: the same normalized string from
at least $k{=}3$ systems, and from at least as many systems as reproduce the gold, that differs
from the gold. Each flagged item falls into one of three cases.
(i)~If the consensus equals the corrupted input, or makes some of the gold corrections and no
other change, this is a \emph{shared miss}, the gold is correct, and we \textbf{keep} the item.
(ii)~If the consensus differs only in punctuation or whitespace, or, in the judged T1 check, is a
register or spelling variant, this is a systematic \emph{over-edit}, the precision signal we want, and we \textbf{keep} it.
(iii)~If the consensus is a \emph{different} spelling fix at an unmarked span, this is a
residual \emph{source} typo, and we \textbf{drop} it. Only case (iii) is a gold defect, and
the explicit test of whether the consensus equals the input prevents conflating it with shared
misses and over-pruning. The audit removed 24 (T1), 26 (T2), and 28 (T3) items, for example
ทั้งสิน$\to$ทั้งสิ้น, ปฏิเศษ$\to$ปฏิเสธ, and โดบ$\to$โดย, all residual \emph{source} typos.
\textbf{How flagged items were checked.} For T2 and T3 the check is automatic and uses the
outputs of all 16 systems: after variant reconciliation, which maps the informal pronoun
เค้า to เขา, a flagged item is dropped unless the consensus equals the input, is a partial fix on
the way to the gold, or differs from the gold only in punctuation or whitespace. The rule cannot
tell a source typo from an accepted variant or register change outside the variant map, so it
also drops a few such items, for example วิกฤติ$\to$วิกฤต, the standard loanword spelling
เซ็ต$\to$เซต, and the informal words พวกเค้า and เท่าไหร่. The T1 audit pooled 9 of the 16 systems
(GPT-5.5 med and high, GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini~3.5
Flash, Claude Sonnet~4.6, DeepSeek~V4 Pro and Flash, GLM-5.1, and Typhoon~2.5), and its flagged
items were judged individually rather than filtered by rule. The audit flagged 70 items. Six differed from the
gold only in punctuation and were kept; we judged the other 64 against the three cases with LLM
assistance, dropping 23 as residual source typos and keeping 41, among them 14 informal-to-formal
rewordings such as มั้ย$\to$ไหม that the minimal-edit contract treats as over-edits. One further
item was dropped because its source sentence itself lacks a syllable (ความใจ for ความเข้าใจ).
Judged and rule-based checks differ at the margin: run on T1 with all 16 systems, the automatic
rule would drop 21 further items. One T1 item and one T2 item contain an error that no model
fixes; both inject แน่นหนา for หนาแน่น, two valid words, so only the context marks the error. We release the audit reports, which list the gold-versus-consensus difference for every
flagged item.

\textbf{Variant map.} The four equivalence groups are an orthographic doublet (สิทธิ/สิทธิ์), two
loanword transliterations (ดิจิตอล/ดิจิทัล and ออร์แกนิค/ออร์แกนิก), and an informal-to-formal
pronoun pair (เค้า/เขา). Words in which the final mark is mandatory, such as ลิขสิทธิ์, are
protected from reconciliation. Beyond these groups, orthographic correction
is essentially single-reference, since a word has one correct spelling, so we do not adopt
multi-reference GEC scoring.

\section{Construction details}
\label{app:construction}
\textbf{Confusion set.} Wrong$\to$correct pairs were harvested from the Thai Wiktionary appendix
of commonly misspelled words and from the fix history of a Thai Wikipedia spelling-cleanup bot,
then deduplicated and cleaned (fragments, multi-token and spacing-only pairs, and
context-dependent real-word pairs removed; one such pair, แน่นหนา/หนาแน่น, was not caught),
giving 1{,}044 canonical pairs. A rule-based
classifier assigns the mechanism class from the character difference; it agrees with 90
hand-labeled bot-fix pairs on 96.5\%. Etymology is assigned from the Wiktionary borrowed-term
categories under the absorption rule, which moves contemporary loans into class~6. We check
canonical forms against the Royal Institute Dictionary word list distributed with PyThaiNLP:
87.5\% are listed, and the unlisted forms include modern loanwords and common words that the list
omits, such as นะคะ. Each pair is also
tagged with its edit operation.
Table~\ref{tab:classcounts} gives the per-class sizes.

\textbf{Injection and mining.} Injection uses \textbf{whole-token-run matching} so the 51 valid
short forms ($\le 3$ characters) that coincide with a confusion target are never corrupted.
Cleanup-bot mining reads the bot's contribution history on Thai Wikipedia and keeps a fix only
when the corrected word is in the Royal Institute Dictionary word list or PyThaiNLP's general
Thai word list and the wrong and correct forms align without shifting neighbouring characters; the mined items are kept out of the three
tiers. The curated subset used in the annotation study keeps at most two contexts per distinct
error and drops fragments of two characters or fewer.

\textbf{Memorization probe.} For each of the four sources we sample 30 clean sentences of 90 to
180 characters, give Typhoon~2.5 (30B) the first half at temperature 0 with the instruction
เติมข้อความต่อไปนี้ให้สมบูรณ์โดยใช้ถ้อยคำเดิมที่ถูกต้องที่สุด ตอบเฉพาะส่วนที่เติมต่อ ห้ามอธิบาย
(`Complete the following text with its exact original wording. Reply with the continuation only,
without explanation.'), and allow up to 120 output tokens. The score is the longest common prefix
between the continuation and the true second half, ignoring whitespace, divided by the length of
the true second half. The mean score is 0.003 (news), 0.004 (government press releases), 0.012
(Wikipedia), and 0.017 (Thai Constitution text, used only in the train split), and no sentence
scores 0.5 or more. The probe uses a single model, so it does not measure memorization by the
other systems directly. Items that share a clean sentence are then kept together as one group,
and groups are assigned to splits class by class (by the class of the group's first item): within
each class, groups are shuffled with a fixed seed and ordered by their source's mean score, and
the lowest-scoring 34\% (mined groups first) form the held-out test split, followed by the public
test (32\%), dev (24\%), and train splits.

\section{Annotation study}
\label{app:annotation}
\textbf{Design.} The 60 items comprise the 40 curated mined items (Appendix~\ref{app:construction})
and 20 synthetic items chosen
so that each automatic class has ten items. Each item shows one marked span with its proposed
correction. Raters answer two questions per item: whether the marked span is a genuine
orthographic error, and which of the six classes it belongs to, following a written guideline
with the class definitions and the two conventions of Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}. No automatic
label is shown. The two raters, one of them an author, worked independently. Etymology and edit-operation tags are not
annotated, since they are a dictionary lookup and a deterministic function of the edit.

\textbf{Results.} Error validity: both raters accepted all 60 spans. Class agreement:
Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.752$ (nominal, $n = 60$). Table~\ref{tab:alpha} gives one-vs-rest
$\alpha$ and raw agreement per automatic class; with ten items per class these values are noisy.

\textbf{Where labels differ.} The raters split on 12 items and agree with each other against the
automatic label on 13. Of these 25, 17 involve the loanword-absorption rule in one of two
directions: raters labeled the surface mechanism of a contemporary loan (for example, a
consonant swap in เวียตนาม$\to$เวียดนาม rated class~2 rather than 6), or recognized a loan that
the loan lexicon missed (เยอรมันนี$\to$เยอรมนี, typed class~5 but rated 6); old borrowings such
as เวทมนตร์ split the raters between class~3 and~6. Four more are homophone swaps outside the
Indic-origin groups (ฅน$\to$คน three times, and กบถ$\to$กบฏ), which the taxonomy places in
class~5 and both raters placed in class~2. The remaining four concern whether a stray or
misplaced diacritic counts as a tone-mark, vowel, or silent-consonant error. The disagreements
therefore sit at the class boundaries fixed by convention rather than at error perception.

\section{Natural-error evaluation}
\label{app:natural}
The natural set comes from the edit history of a Thai Wikipedia spelling-cleanup bot: 103 items, each a
real error in its original encyclopedic sentence, with the bot's fix as gold. We apply the consensus
audit of Section~\ref{sec:qc} with the same rule as the tiers: at least $k = 3$ systems, and at
least as many as reproduce the gold, must agree on an output that differs from the gold. The audit
drops an item when that output makes a spelling change the gold does not, and keeps it when the
output equals the input, differs only in whitespace or punctuation, or is a partial fix on the way
to the gold. It removes 16 items. In some, the consensus also fixes a second, unmarked error in the
sentence (for example เพึย$\to$เพีย); in others, the recorded fix is itself incomplete and the
consensus completes it (ตำ่ recorded as ตำ, น้้า as น้า, and สรา้ง as สราง, where the intended words
are ต่ำ, น้ำ, and สร้าง). Dropping an
item whenever three systems agree, however many reproduce the gold, would remove 24 items
(79 kept; mean correction F1 0.81, $\rho = 0.92$), including one where the systems change a
correctly spelled film title. The audited set has 87 items with 87 recorded fixes (88 aligned gold edits). Classified without
the loanword rule, the fixes fall in classes 1: 27, 2: 21, 3: 14, 4: 20, and 5: 5; the edit-typer,
which applies the rule, types 7 of the 88 gold edits as loanwords. Table~\ref{tab:natural} gives per-model results on the audited

\section{Prompt and inference}
\label{app:prompt}
\textbf{Prompt.} All models receive the same Thai system message and instruction. The system
message is คุณเป็นเครื่องมือตรวจแก้การสะกดคำภาษาไทยตามอักขรวิธีมาตรฐานของราชบัณฑิตยสภา (`You are
a tool that checks and corrects Thai spelling according to the standard orthography of the Royal
Society'). The T1 instruction, with the input in place of \{text\}, is:
\begin{quote}\small\raggedright
จงแก้คำที่สะกดผิดในประโยคต่อไปนี้ให้ถูกต้องตามอักขรวิธี แก้เฉพาะตัวพยัญชนะ/สระ/วรรณยุกต์/ตัวการันต์ที่สะกดผิดเท่านั้น
ห้ามเปลี่ยนถ้อยคำ ห้ามเรียบเรียงใหม่ ห้ามเพิ่มหรือลบเนื้อความ ห้ามเปลี่ยนการเว้นวรรค ตัวเลข
หรือเครื่องหมายวรรคตอน ถ้าไม่มีคำผิดให้ตอบประโยคเดิมทุกตัวอักษร
ตอบกลับเป็นประโยคที่แก้ไขแล้วบรรทัดเดียวเท่านั้น ไม่ต้องอธิบาย\\[3pt]
ประโยค: \{text\}\\
ประโยคที่แก้ไขแล้ว:
\end{quote}
For T2 and T3, ประโยค (`sentence') becomes ข้อความ (`text'), and the single-line requirement is
dropped. The English prompt of the sensitivity test below translates the T1 instruction:
\begin{quote}\small\raggedright
Correct the misspelled Thai words in the following sentence according to standard Thai
orthography. Fix only misspelled consonants, vowels, tone marks, or silent-consonant (karan)
marks. Do not change the wording, do not rephrase, do not add or remove content, and do not
change spacing, numerals, or punctuation. If there are no misspellings, return the sentence
exactly as given. Reply with only the corrected sentence on a single line, with no
explanation.\\[3pt]
Sentence: \{text\}\\
Corrected sentence:
\end{quote}
The few-shot prompt keeps the Thai instruction and inserts ตัวอย่าง (`Examples') followed by two
worked examples from the train split, in the same ประโยค / ประโยคที่แก้ไขแล้ว format, before the
input. The examples contain a tone-mark error (ก้อ$\to$ก็) and a consonant-pair error
(ฐาณ$\to$ฐาน):
\begin{quote}\small\raggedright
ประโยค: แต่เพลงนี้ก้อยังไม่ได้มีการประกาศอย่างเป็นทางการว่าเป็นเพลงชาติ\\
ประโยคที่แก้ไขแล้ว: แต่เพลงนี้ก็ยังไม่ได้มีการประกาศอย่างเป็นทางการว่าเป็นเพลงชาติ\\[3pt]
ประโยค: โดยถ้าลอการิทึมในสมการข้างต้น เป็นฐาณสอง เอนโทรปีที่วัดจะอยู่ในหน่วยบิตเช่นกัน\\
ประโยคที่แก้ไขแล้ว: โดยถ้าลอการิทึมในสมการข้างต้น เป็นฐานสอง เอนโทรปีที่วัดจะอยู่ในหน่วยบิตเช่นกัน
\end{quote}
Both variants keep the Thai system message.

\textbf{Inference.} Temperature is 0 where supported. Claude
models use the Anthropic Message Batches API in non-thinking mode. GPT-5.x models use the
Responses API, GPT-5.5 at medium or high reasoning effort with 3k and 6k output-token budgets and
GPT-5.4-mini at its default effort with a 2k budget. Open reasoning models run
in native thinking mode with large token budgets, and token-runout counts as a failure. Replies
are cleaned by removing reasoning traces and echoed labels, keeping the last non-empty line for T1
or joining all lines for T2 and T3, and stripping quotation marks at both ends. For the 127 of
18{,}256 scored replies (0.7\%) whose passage itself begins or ends with a quotation mark, this
cleaning also removes a correct quotation mark, which counts as one error. Scoring these replies
without the stripping changes any model's correction F1 by at most 0.005 and leaves the order of
models unchanged in every tier.

\textbf{Prompt sensitivity.} We rerun the full T1 set for one strong general model (Gemma-4-31B)
and one Thai 8B model (Typhoon-S) under two alternative prompts: the same instruction in English,
and the Thai instruction with two worked examples from the train split, which is disjoint from T1 (Table~\ref{tab:prompt}).
Prompt choice shifts correction F1 by at most 0.07, and Gemma-4 stays ahead of Typhoon-S under
every prompt. The two models move in opposite directions: the worked examples help the Thai 8B
model and hurt the general model.
